From 400 to 10,000 operations per second in CamusDB

October 22, 2026 · 7 min read
Andres Gutierrez
Creator of CamusDB

If you're investigating database throughput, our experience offers a practical place to look: the work repeated around every query and commit. Here's what we removed, how we measured the gains, and what the resulting benchmark can tell you about CamusDB.

Many months ago, when we first started measuring database throughput in cluster mode, we discovered it could only handle around 400 ops/sec. After a long journey of benchmarking, investigating bottlenecks, and optimizing the system, we've reached 10K ops/sec.

On October 12, a three-node cluster sustained 10,284 durable operations per second for 45 minutes, completing 27.8 million operations after a two-minute warm-up. Every measured minute stayed above 9,700 ops/sec.

All three nodes ran on one laptop, alongside the load generator and metrics collector. And the storage was memory-backed. Both details matter when interpreting the number.

A young database meets the Join Order Benchmark

October 18, 2026 · 9 min read
Andres Gutierrez
Creator of CamusDB

If your queries join five, ten or fifteen tables, CamusDB now plans them better and runs them faster. This post explains what changed in plain terms, so you can see what to expect from your own queries.

A join query names its tables and its conditions. It does not say in which order to join the tables. The database picks the order, and the order decides how much data the query reads. A good order starts from the few rows that survive the filters. A bad order can build millions of rows and then throw most of them away.

The Join Order Benchmark (JOB) is a set of 113 queries from a 2015 paper by Leis and five co-authors. The queries run over a movie database of 21 tables, and each one joins 4 to 17 tables. The paper tested mature databases and found that their estimates of how many rows a join returns were often wrong by orders of magnitude. The error grows with each join, and a wrong estimate leads to a slow plan. The benchmark exists to show that weakness.

A ten-minute test finds the bugs that happen in ten minutes.

A distributed database is hard to build, and the code is the smaller half of the work. The larger half is verification. You check the results again and again, you dig into the system until you know what it really does, and you push it past the point where it should break. A guarantee on a documentation page is worth exactly what the evidence behind it can show, and that evidence has to cover speed and correctness at the same time.

Part one introduced Caraxes, the harness that attacks a real CamusDB cluster in Docker. Part two told what it found in its first month: a dropped transfer leg, a cluster that said healthy while it was dead, scans that lost rows under load.

This part covers the three weeks after that. Most of it is about one test. It runs for two hours, it injects a fault every eight minutes, and it took eight attempts before it passed. Every failed attempt found something, and the runs after the first pass kept finding things.

Teaching CamusDB to choose better query plans

July 11, 2026 · 9 min read
Andres Gutierrez
Creator of CamusDB

One of the most interesting parts of building CamusDB is the query optimizer. It might also be one of the most complex pieces of machinery in the whole database.

At first, a query optimizer sounds simple: receive a SQL query and decide how to run it. But the more features a database supports, the harder that decision becomes. A query can use a table scan, an index lookup, an index range scan, a hash join, a merge join, a nested loop join, a sort, an aggregate, a derived table, or a subquery. Each option can be correct, but not every option is fast.

The optimizer's job is to choose a good plan before the query runs.

Why CamusDB exists

July 3, 2026 · 6 min read
Andres Gutierrez
Creator of CamusDB

Perfection is achieved, not when there is nothing more to add, but when there is nothing left to take away.

— Antoine de Saint-Exupéry

For many years I worked with Google Spanner, and somewhere along the way I fell in love with distributed SQL databases.

That may sound a little too dramatic for a database, but honestly, it is true.

In the video game industry, databases are not just boring infrastructure sitting in the background. They are a critical part of the player experience. Games need to handle spikes, global traffic, competitive systems, economies, inventories, matchmaking, leaderboards, live events, rewards, and a lot of things that must work correctly even when thousands or millions of players are active.

That is where distributed SQL really clicked for me.

Strong consistency matters. Scalability matters. Being able to reason about your data without constantly adding workarounds, queues, caches, compensating jobs, or manual repair scripts matters a lot. Spanner showed me that a database could solve many of these problems at the infrastructure level instead of forcing every backend team to reinvent the same patterns over and over again.

At some point, I started asking myself: what would it look like to build something like this myself?

Not because the world needs yet another database just for the sake of it, but because I wanted to explore the architecture deeply. I wanted to understand the tradeoffs, the hard parts, the abstractions, and the places where I could experiment with ideas that I think are useful.

That is how CamusDB started.
