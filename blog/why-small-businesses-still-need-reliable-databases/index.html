Why Small Businesses Still Need Reliable Databases

October 6, 2026 · 9 min read
Andres Gutierrez
Creator of CamusDB

When people hear the words "distributed SQL database," they usually imagine very large companies. They think about millions of users, thousands of servers, huge amounts of data, and engineering teams operating infrastructure across several regions.

Because of this, it is easy to assume that distributed databases are only useful when a company reaches a certain scale.

But I think this misses an important part of the problem.

A small business may have a small workload, but that does not mean its data is less important.

Imagine a small online store processing 500 orders per day. From a database perspective, this is almost nothing. A single PostgreSQL server can easily handle that workload, probably without using much of its available CPU.

But what happens when that server fails?

The business still needs to know which customers paid, which orders were completed, how much inventory remains, and which transactions were confirmed. Losing ten transactions can be insignificant for a company processing billions of operations, but it can be a serious problem for a small business.

The interesting question, then, is not whether small businesses need massive database scalability. Most of them do not.

The question is whether they need reliability.

And I think many of them do.

Small Workload Does Not Mean Small Reliability Requirements​

There is an important difference between performance requirements and reliability requirements.

A small business may only execute a few database transactions per second. That workload can run comfortably on a small machine. However, the business may still need strong guarantees around those transactions.

If a customer completes a payment, that payment should not disappear because a server crashed a few seconds later. If two customers try to buy the last available product, the database should correctly decide who gets it. If a machine dies during the night, ideally the entire business should not remain offline until somebody wakes up and fixes it.

These are not "big company problems."

They are normal business problems.

The difference is that large companies usually have more resources to deal with them. They may have database administrators, infrastructure engineers, monitoring systems, incident procedures, and engineers available 24/7.

A small company may have two developers.

One of them might also be responsible for the frontend, backend, database, deployment pipeline, monitoring, and answering Slack messages when something breaks.

For that company, infrastructure that can recover automatically can be extremely valuable.

Distributed SQL Is Not Only About Scaling​

Distributed databases are often presented as a solution to horizontal scaling.

That is certainly one of their advantages. If one machine cannot handle the workload anymore, the system can distribute data and work across several machines.

But distribution can solve another problem that is sometimes more important: failure.

Machines fail. Disks fail. Networks become unreliable. Processes crash. Cloud instances disappear. Software has bugs.

A distributed database can keep multiple copies of the same data and use consensus protocols such as Raft to decide which operations have been safely committed.

If one node disappears, the remaining nodes can continue operating as long as they still have a quorum.

This means that distribution is not necessarily about making a database faster.

Sometimes it is about making failure boring.

And boring failures are incredibly valuable.

There Is a Catch​

Of course, distributed systems are not free.

A three-node database cluster requires more infrastructure than a single PostgreSQL instance. Replication consumes network bandwidth. Consensus adds latency. Maintaining multiple copies of data requires additional storage.

There is also much more complexity inside the database itself.

This creates an interesting tradeoff.

A distributed database can be much more complicated internally while making infrastructure much simpler for the people using it.

That distinction between internal complexity and operational complexity is important.

As a developer, I do not necessarily care if the database internally uses Raft, replication logs, fencing tokens, hybrid logical clocks, or distributed transactions. What I care about is whether I need to understand all of those things to operate my application.

The ideal database hides most of that complexity.

If a node dies, another node should take over. If traffic increases, capacity should increase. If traffic decreases, infrastructure costs should decrease. If data becomes corrupted or accidentally deleted, recovery should be possible.

The database can be extremely sophisticated internally while presenting a relatively simple experience to the developer.

But PostgreSQL Already Solves Many of These Problems​

This is where I think discussions about distributed databases sometimes become unfair.

PostgreSQL is extremely good.

For many small businesses, a managed PostgreSQL service is probably the correct choice. It can provide backups, replicas, monitoring, automatic failover, and many other features without requiring the company to operate PostgreSQL itself.

You do not need a distributed SQL database just because distributed databases exist.

If your application fits comfortably on one machine, occasional failover is acceptable, and your managed provider handles most operational work, PostgreSQL can remain a very good solution for a very long time.

The argument for distributed SQL becomes stronger when the alternative starts requiring additional infrastructure around the database.

Maybe you need read replicas. Then you need connection routing. Later you need sharding. Now the application needs to understand where data lives. Perhaps you introduce Redis for distributed locks. Then you need another highly available system. Eventually you add queues, background workers, failover procedures, and several monitoring systems.

None of these technologies are bad.

The problem is that operational complexity can grow gradually until a relatively simple application has become a small distributed system built around a database that was originally chosen because it was simple.

Small Companies Have a Different Scaling Problem​

When engineers talk about scalability, we usually talk about requests per second, storage capacity, or number of users.

There is another resource that needs to scale: engineering time.

A company with three engineers cannot operate infrastructure the same way a company with three hundred engineers can.

This makes operational simplicity particularly valuable for small teams.

If the database can automatically handle replication, failover, backups, recovery, and eventually horizontal scaling, the company can spend more engineering time building its product instead of building infrastructure around its database.

In that sense, distributed SQL can be useful even when the actual database workload is tiny.

The database is not scaling because the company has millions of queries per second.

It is scaling the capabilities of a small engineering team.

Cost Still Matters​

There is one major obstacle to this idea: distributed databases can be expensive.

If running a reliable database requires three relatively large servers at all times, that may not make sense for a company with a small workload.

This is why I think the future of distributed databases is not only about improving their maximum throughput.

It is also about making the minimum deployment smaller and cheaper.

Ideally, a company should be able to start small and grow without changing databases.

Maybe it starts with a very small deployment. Later it enables additional replicas for better availability. As traffic increases, more nodes can join the cluster. As traffic decreases, unnecessary capacity can disappear.

The important part is that the application does not need to be redesigned every time the infrastructure changes.

You should not need to choose a database for the company you hope to become five years from now.

You should be able to choose a database that grows with you.

This Is One of the Ideas Behind CamusDB​

This question is particularly interesting to me because I have been working on CamusDB, a distributed SQL database built on top of Kahuna, my distributed coordination and data system.

When building a distributed database, it is very easy to focus on impressive benchmark numbers. Transactions per second are easy to show. Latency graphs are easy to compare. Large clusters are interesting engineering problems.

But I increasingly think that scalability alone is not the most interesting part.

The more interesting goal is making the guarantees normally associated with complex distributed infrastructure available without requiring every company to build that infrastructure itself.

Strong consistency should not only be useful to banks.

Automatic recovery should not only be useful to companies with hundreds of engineers.

Horizontal scaling should not require rewriting half of an application.

And surviving a server failure should not require somebody waking up at 3 AM.

Of course, achieving this is much harder than describing it. Distributed systems introduce their own failure modes, and a distributed database that constantly requires human intervention is not an improvement over a simpler database.

The complexity has to live inside the system instead of leaking into the application.

That is the difficult part.

Maybe We Are Asking the Wrong Question​

So, does every small business need a distributed SQL database?

No.

For many applications, a traditional relational database will work perfectly well.

But I think asking whether a small company "needs distributed SQL" focuses too much on the technology.

A better question is what guarantees the business needs.

Does it need to survive machine failures? Does it need transactions that cannot simply disappear? Does it need automatic recovery? Does it want to grow without eventually introducing sharding? Does it have enough engineers to operate all the infrastructure required to provide those guarantees?

Those questions have very little to do with how many requests per second the application currently receives.

A company processing 20 transactions per second can care just as much about those 20 transactions as a company processing 200,000.

Small businesses do not necessarily need distributed databases.

They need reliable databases that are affordable, understandable, and easy to operate.

If distributed SQL can provide those properties without forcing small teams to pay the traditional cost and complexity of distributed infrastructure, then I think it has a much bigger audience than we normally imagine.
